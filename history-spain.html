The Reconquista

Spain before it was Spain. In 711 an army crossed from Africa and took the whole peninsula in seven years, except one scrap of mountain. What grew out of that scrap took seven hundred and seventy years to finish the job. Click everything on this page. Most of it opens.

1Start here: a scrap of mountain

Before any names or dates, stand where the story starts.

The year is 718. Seven years ago an army crossed the strait from Africa, killed your king in one battle, and took every city in the peninsula. The Visigothic kingdom that ruled this land for three hundred years is gone. Its nobles have submitted, converted, or fled.

You are in the mountains of Asturias, on the north coast, with the men who refused. Everything south of you belongs to the emir. You hold a few hundred square miles of rock, rain, and cattle. Nobody is coming to help.

How does a scrap of mountain become a country?

The point to write down

Asturias is the seed. Everything Christian on the map after 722 grows from this scrap of mountain: Asturias becomes León, León's frontier county becomes Castile, and beside them Navarre, Aragon, and Portugal take shape. That is why one small place with a hard-to-spell name matters far beyond its size.

Keep one question running as you scroll: at each step, what did men do, and what did they say God did?

2Iberia is not Spain (yet)

There is no Spain in this story until the last page. There is a peninsula, Iberia, with four layers of rulers laid down on it before this story begins. Click a layer.

Rome called the peninsula Hispania and held it for six hundred years. It gave the land its roads, its cities, its law, and its languages: Spanish, Portuguese, and Catalan are all Latin that kept changing. Two emperors, Trajan and Hadrian, were born here, and so was the philosopher Seneca, at Córdoba.

As Rome failed, Germanic peoples came over the Pyrenees: the Vandals first, then the Visigoths, who ruled as a small warrior elite over a large Roman population for three centuries, with their capital at Toledo.

They were Christians until the concerted work of St. Leander and St. Isidore of Seville, with Pope St. Gregory the Great behind them, won the kingdom for the Creed. King Reccared professed the Catholic faith at the Third Council of Toledo in 589.

In 711 Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed the strait with an army of under Arab command. The rock where he landed still carries his name: Gibraltar, from Jabal Tariq, Tariq's mountain. At the river Guadalete he defeated and killed King Roderic, the last Visigothic king. The kingdom's nobles fell to fighting each other over the succession, and city after city surrendered on terms.

By 718 the conquest was complete except for a couple hundred square miles in the mountains of Asturias. The conquerors called their new land al-Andalus.

al-Andalus is the Arabic name for Islamic Iberia, whatever its size at the moment. Its capital was Córdoba. It was ruled in turn by governors sent from Damascus (711–756), by an independent (756–929), by a (929–1031), then by a scatter of small rival kingdoms called (1031–1086), then by two waves of Berber rulers from Morocco, the Almoravids (1086) and the Almohads (1147), and at last by the emirs of Granada alone (1238–1492).

Its people were Muslims, Christians (called Mozarabs, Arabized Christians), and Jews, living under Islamic law side by side and, periodically, at each other's expense. Section 7 returns to them.

4Pelayo and Covadonga

The man the scrap of mountain chose. Read the story, then sort it.

Asturias was overrun in 714. The people who would not submit went higher into the mountains. Among them was a man the Latin sources call Pelagius and Spain calls Don Pelayo. He had been captured by the Muslims and had escaped. The men in the mountains elected him their war chief, and he gathered the scattered resistance under one banner.

About 722 a column was sent to finish the holdouts. At Covadonga, at a cave in the mountains, Pelayo's men beat it. The victory was small in numbers and enormous in consequence: it turned a refusal into a state. Pelayo's family ruled it after him, and it grew, under his son-in-law Alfonso I and his descendants, into the Kingdom of Asturias, reaching eventually from the Atlantic to the Pyrenees. Later rulers broke it into pieces that became León, Castile, and Navarre, which grew into kingdoms of their own.

In Spanish memory Pelayo is a sort of King Arthur meets Robin Hood, an outlaw leader fighting for justice and a noble ruler anointed by God, both at once. Later sources call him king; the earliest one calls him an elected war chief. Every later Iberian dynasty descends from him.

5From the seed: the five kingdoms

Click a card.

Founder
Don Pelayo, elected war chief about 722. The Asturian progenitor of all the rest.
What happened
Grew under Alfonso I (Galicia) and Alfonso III the Great (the Duero frontier: Porto, Burgos, Zamora). After Alfonso III, the last king before the name changed, a three-way civil war among his sons ended with the kingdom re-centred on the city of León (924).
Then
León and Castile were joined and split repeatedly until 1230, when they were united for good.
The name
The land of castles: the fortified eastern frontier of Asturias-León, facing the Muslim raids up the Duero.
Founder as a kingdom
Ferdinand I, 1035. A kingdom proper by the time of his son Sancho II, El Cid's first king.
Then
United with León in 1230. Its dialect, Castilian, is the basis of modern Spanish, with the famous lisp on certain c and z sounds.
Founder
Íñigo Arista, a Basque chieftain, after a victory over the Franks at the second battle of Roncevaux (824). The kingdom of Pamplona first; Navarre later.
Greatest ruler
Sancho III Garcés, the Great (r. 1004–1035): small Navarre briefly dominated both Iberia and the Pyrenees (992–1035), and he founded the kingdom of Aragon for his son.
Then
Precariously perched between Iberia and France. Absorbed into French rule in 1610 when the king of Navarre became king of France, keeping a half-autonomy into the 1800s.
Origins
A county of the Frankish empire in the high Pyrenees, then conquered by Sancho the Great of Navarre, who gave it to his son Ramiro I as a new kingdom in 1035.
Growth
Alfonso I the Battler took Zaragoza (1118). Alfonso II the Chaste added Catalonia and Barcelona (the union of 1137). The Crown of Aragon then looked east, to the sea.
Greatest ruler
James I the Conqueror (r. 1213–1276): Mallorca, Valencia, and a Mediterranean empire. He closes the story of the early kingdoms.
The name
From Portus Cale, the harbour at the mouth of the Douro, today's Porto. Founded by Vímara Peres in the 860s as a county of Asturias.
Founder as a kingdom
Afonso Henriques, who defeated his own mother's forces at São Mamede (1128) for autonomy from León, won at Ourique (1139) and was proclaimed king, and took Lisbon (1147) with the help of crusaders sailing to the Holy Land.
Then
Complete to the Algarve by 1249. The first of the Iberian kingdoms to finish its Reconquista, and the one that then turned to the Atlantic.
Not one of the five kingdoms
Because it was never a kingdom. Charlemagne's Franks took Girona (785) and Barcelona (801) and ruled the Pyrenean counties as the Spanish March. The counts of Barcelona slipped free of the French kings and in 1137 joined their lands to Aragon by marriage.
Why it matters
It is where the Crown of Aragon got its coast, its fleet, and its second language, Catalan, which James I wrote his memoirs in.

6El Cid: whose side is he on?

Rodrigo Díaz de Vivar, c. 1043–1099. Step through his career and keep a tally. Then read his death in the source, binder pp. 19–24.

The names

El Cid, from Arabic as-Sayyid, the lord. Campeador, from Latin campidoctor, master of the field. Two swords, Tizona and Colada. A horse, Babieca. A wife, Jimena.

The record

Never lost a battle. Served two Christian kings, two Islamic rulers, and himself. Prince of Valencia 1094–1099.

The source

The Chronicle of the Cid, an anonymous Castilian compilation of the 1200s, translated by Robert Southey in 1808. Binder pp. 19–24. It cites an Arabic historian, Abenalfarax, three times. Ask why.

Reading the death ride, pp. 19–24

Read it with the Five Ws in the margin and at least two questions of your own. These are the places to slow down. The answers are yours to find, not the page's to give.

  1. p. 19. The Cid gives orders for his own body: washed, anointed, dressed, tied upright on Babieca with Tizona in his hand. Whose idea is the ride, in this telling? What does that change about how you read what follows?
  2. p. 20. The testament: money to the knights, cloth for four thousand poor, the sacraments, the prayer, "pure and without spot." Which lines are about a soldier, and which are about a saint? Who needed him to die like this?
  3. pp. 21–22. Thirty-six kings, fifteen thousand tents, a Moorish queen and her two hundred archers. Then the exit at midnight and the charge. Mark the exact sentence where the account stops being something a witness could have seen.
  4. p. 22. "Seventy thousand knights, all as white as snow," led by a knight on a white horse with a bloody cross. Who is that supposed to be? Who says they saw it, and whose report is the chronicle passing on?
  5. p. 23. The Arabic writer Abenalfarax, the Moors' amazement, the writing on the wall, and the last line: Valencia stays Moorish 170 years "till it was won by King Don Jayme of Aragon." The source ends by introducing the man in the Aragon card above. Why would a Castilian chronicle end a Castilian hero's story with a king of Aragon?
A comparison, if the argument stalls

King Arthur and Robin Hood are myths with a kernel of fact. George Washington and Abraham Lincoln are facts wearing myths. Which is El Cid? Decide, and say what evidence would change your mind.

7Splintered light

In Reconquista Iberia, morality was often discarded for ambition, on both sides of the religious divide. It was also a place of intense scholarship and art. Both have to be held at once.

The road back to Aristotle

Most of Aristotle was lost to the Latin West for centuries. It survived in Arabic, was argued over by Muslim and Jewish philosophers in Córdoba (Averroes, Maimonides) and the East (Avicenna), and came back into Latin largely through the translators of Toledo after 1085. The Aristotle St. Thomas Aquinas argued with at Paris travelled that road. Aquinas's Prayer Before Study has an Iberian back story.

The Alhambra

The palace of the emirs of Granada, built mostly in the 1300s by the last Islamic state in Iberia, and the most beautiful building on the peninsula. Ferdinand and Isabella took it in 1492 and, to their credit, did not pull it down.

Living together

How Muslims, Christians, and Jews actually lived together under Islamic law, and what changed when the Almoravids and Almohads arrived: the dhimmi code, sparingly used for three centuries and then enforced hard. An old saying for the whole picture: God draws straight with crooked lines.

8The dates to know

Seven. Click one for the reason it is on the list.

Tariq ibn Ziyad defeats King Roderic. The Visigothic kingdom falls in one battle; the conquest is complete by 718.
Pelayo's victory in the Asturian mountains. The traditional first day of the Reconquista and the seed of every later kingdom.
Charles Martel stops the Islamic advance in France. The Pyrenees become the frontier, and the Franks turn back south to carve out the Spanish March.
Alfonso VI takes the old Visigothic capital. The next year the Almoravids land in answer, and El Cid's world is made.
Alfonso VIII of Castile, with Aragon, Navarre, and Portugal, breaks the Almohads on 16 July. The door to the south opens.
The great advance: Ferdinand III takes Córdoba and Seville, James I takes Valencia. Only Granada is left, for 250 years.
The last Islamic state surrenders to Ferdinand and Isabella on 2 January. Columbus sails in August. Now there is a Spain.

Say it as one breath: 711, 722, 732, 1085, 1212, 1248, 1492. Two beginnings, one stop, one turn, one break, one sweep, one end.

Terms

IberiaThe peninsula, from the Greek name for the river Ebro. The land, whoever holds it.
HispaniaThe Roman name for Iberia, and the ancestor of the word Spain.
VisigothsThe Germanic people who ruled Iberia c. 420–711 from Toledo, Arian until 589.
ArianThe heresy that the Son is less than the Father. The Goths' Christianity until Leander, Isidore, and Gregory the Great.
GuadaleteThe river and battle of 711 where King Roderic fell to Tariq ibn Ziyad.
al-AndalusArabic name for Islamic Iberia, whatever its size. Capital Córdoba.
MoorMedieval Christian word for the Muslims of Iberia and North Africa. From Latin Mauri.
BerberThe native peoples of North Africa, the bulk of the army of 711 and of the Almoravids and Almohads later.
Emir, emirateCommander, prince; a ruler who does not claim to lead all Islam. Córdoba was an emirate 756–929.
Caliph, caliphateSuccessor of Muhammad, leader of all Muslims. Córdoba claimed the title 929–1031.
TaifaFaction. One of the small rival kingdoms al-Andalus broke into after 1031, each named for its city.
AlmoravidsBerber reformers from Morocco who landed in 1086, beat Alfonso VI, and swallowed the taifas.
AlmohadsThe stricter Berber movement that overthrew the Almoravids and crossed to Iberia in 1147. Broken at Las Navas de Tolosa, 1212.
CovadongaThe cave in the Asturian mountains where Pelayo won about 722.
ReconquistaReconquest. The later name for the Christian kingdoms' 770-year advance, 722–1492.
CampeadorMaster of the field. El Cid's battlefield title, from Latin campidoctor.
as-SayyidArabic, the lord. Said by Muslims and Christians alike of Rodrigo Díaz; in Castilian, El Cid.
MozarabsChristians living under Islamic rule in al-Andalus, Arabic-speaking and keeping their own rite.
DhimmiNon-Muslims (Christians and Jews) protected under Islamic law, with restrictions. See section 7.
